Trusted sources

Where to get real help

These are official government agencies and well-known nonprofits we recommend. Every one is free to use.

A safety tip for this page: scammers sometimes pretend to be these same organizations. Only trust a phone number or website you looked up yourself, like the ones below. None of these groups will ask you to pay with gift cards or move money to “protect” it.

Call for help

Talk to a person now

If something feels wrong, or you think you or someone you love was scammed, these helplines are free and staffed by trained people.

  • National Elder Fraud Hotline

    Run by the U.S. Department of Justice for adults 60 and older. A case manager helps you report what happened and connects you with other help. Family members can call too.

    833-FRAUD-11 (833-372-8311) ovc.ojp.gov (opens in a new tab)

  • AARP Fraud Watch Network Helpline

    Free for everyone, not just AARP members. Trained volunteers help you decide if something is a scam and what to do next.

    Monday to Friday, 8 a.m. to 8 p.m. Eastern

    877-908-3360 aarp.org/fraudhelpline (opens in a new tab)

  • Eldercare Locator

    A public service of the U.S. Administration for Community Living. It connects older adults and caregivers with trusted local services, including help after fraud or abuse.

    800-677-1116 eldercare.acl.gov (opens in a new tab)

Report a scam

Tell the authorities

Reporting helps stop scammers and warn others, even if you did not lose any money.

  • Federal Trade Commission (FTC)

    The main place to report any scam, bad business practice, or fraud in the United States.

    ReportFraud.ftc.gov (opens in a new tab)

  • FBI Internet Crime Complaint Center (IC3)

    Report scams that happened online, by email, or through a website, including fake tech support and investment scams.

    ic3.gov (opens in a new tab)

  • Social Security scams

    If someone pretends to be from Social Security or its Inspector General, report it here. Real Social Security problems usually come by mail.

    oig.ssa.gov/report (opens in a new tab)

  • Your state attorney general

    Every state has a consumer protection office that takes complaints. This directory, from the National Association of Attorneys General, links to yours.

    Find your state’s office (opens in a new tab)

  • Not sure where to report?

    USA.gov, the government’s official guide, asks a few questions and points you to the right agency.

    usa.gov/where-report-scams (opens in a new tab)

Protect your identity

If your information or money was taken

Act quickly. Start by calling your bank using the number on the back of your card.

  • IdentityTheft.gov

    Run by the FTC. Tell it what happened and it builds a step-by-step recovery plan, with letters you can send to banks and businesses.

    877-438-4338 IdentityTheft.gov (opens in a new tab)

  • Freeze your credit

    A credit freeze is free and stops anyone from opening new accounts in your name. Contact all three credit bureaus.

    Equifax: 800-685-1111
    Experian: 888-397-3742
    TransUnion: 888-909-8872

    FTC guide to credit freezes (opens in a new tab)

Fewer scam calls

Cut down on unwanted calls

Fewer calls means fewer chances for a scammer to reach you.

  • National Do Not Call Registry

    Free, from the FTC. Sales calls should stop within 31 days. It will not stop every scammer, but it makes any sales call you still get more suspicious.

    Call from the phone you want to register.

    888-382-1222 DoNotCall.gov (opens in a new tab)

Keep learning

Stay up to date on new scams

Scams change all the time. These sites post plain-language warnings about the newest ones.

  • FTC Consumer Advice

    Short articles on the latest scams and how to avoid them. You can sign up for free email alerts.

    consumer.ftc.gov/scams (opens in a new tab)

  • AARP Fraud Watch Network

    Scam alerts, a map of scams reported near you, and free support groups for people affected by fraud.

    aarp.org/scams (opens in a new tab)

  • Consumer Financial Protection Bureau

    Free guides and handouts to help older adults and caregivers protect their money.

    consumerfinance.gov (opens in a new tab)

  • Social Security: scam warnings

    The four signs of a Social Security scam and what to do if you were targeted.

    ssa.gov/scam (opens in a new tab)

We last checked these phone numbers and links in November 2026. Spot something out of date? Let us know.
